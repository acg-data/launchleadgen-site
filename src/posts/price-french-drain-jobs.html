Most drainage contractors lose bids because they priced high without explaining why, or they win jobs at a price that eats the margin. Build the number from your costs, give the homeowner options, and present it so they compare scope before they compare price.

Key takeaways

  • Build every price from five cost buckets: labor, materials, equipment, disposal and restoration, and overhead plus profit.
  • Use per-linear-foot pricing to check your number, and don't quote it as your price.
  • Offer good/better/best options so the homeowner picks between your packages instead of between you and a competitor.
  • Present in person or on a call, explain the problem first and show the price last.

The five cost buckets

A per-foot number remembered from an old job is a guess. Every French drain or yard drainage job breaks into the same five buckets. Write them out on every bid, including the small ones.

1. Labor

Estimate crew hours for layout and locates, trenching, fabric and pipe, stone, backfill and cleanup. Multiply by your loaded labor rate: wages plus payroll taxes, workers' comp and benefits. A worker you pay $22 an hour costs you noticeably more once those are added. If you don't know your loaded rate, work it out before the next bid.

2. Materials

Perforated pipe, solid pipe for discharge, filter fabric, washed stone, fittings, catch basins, pop-up emitters, dry well parts. Price from current supplier invoices and add a waste factor for stone and fabric. You will almost always use more than the plan says.

3. Equipment

Mini excavator, trencher, track loader, dump trailer. Charge for machines you own too, since they carry payments, maintenance, fuel and wear. Set an internal hourly or daily rate for each one and include it on every job that uses it.

4. Disposal and restoration

This is the bucket people forget, and it is where margin disappears: dump fees and spoil hauling, seed or sod, resetting pavers you pulled, repairing irrigation lines. If the yard has a sprinkler system, assume you will cut at least one line and budget for the repair.

5. Overhead and profit

Overhead covers what keeps the business running when nobody is digging: truck payments, insurance, phones, software, office help, marketing and lead costs. Profit is a separate line. It pays you for the risk and buys the next machine, so don't drop it because a homeowner pushes back.

Put your marketing cost per sold job into overhead. If you pay for leads, that cost belongs in the price the same way stone and pipe do.

Per-foot vs. job-based pricing

Homeowners often ask, "What do you charge per foot?" Answering with one number is a mistake, because footage leaves out most of what drives the cost.

ApproachWhere it helpsWhere it hurts
Per linear footQuick internal check; fine for simple, open-yard runsIgnores access, depth, soil, discharge and restoration; invites apples-to-oranges comparisons
Job-based (scope price)Captures the cost of the specific site; easier to defendTakes longer to build; needs a clear written scope

Price the job from the five buckets, then divide by the footage to get your effective per-foot number. If it lands far outside your usual range, look again. Either the site is unusual (tight access, rocky soil, deep pipe, a long discharge run) or you missed something.

Worked example

This is an illustration. Your costs will differ, and prices vary by region.

  • Run: 90 feet of French drain along a side yard, discharging to the curb
  • Labor: 3-person crew, 1.5 days, 36 total hours at a $40 loaded rate = $1,440
  • Materials: pipe, fabric, stone, fittings, curb outlet = $1,050
  • Equipment: mini excavator, 1.5 days at an internal rate of $300/day = $450
  • Disposal and restoration: spoil haul, seed and straw, one irrigation repair = $400
  • Direct cost subtotal: $3,340
  • Overhead at 20% of direct cost: $668
  • Profit at 15% of cost plus overhead: about $601
  • Price: about $4,610, or roughly $51 per foot effective

Say a competitor quotes "$35 a foot," which is $3,150. If you only talk per foot, you look $1,460 more expensive. If you talk scope, you may find their number leaves out the curb outlet, the restoration and the irrigation repair, and the homeowner will find that out later.

Good, better and best options

With one price, the homeowner decides between you and another contractor. With three, they decide which of your options they want.

OptionWhat it includesWho it's for
GoodFrench drain along the problem area, standard pipe and stone, discharge to the nearest acceptable point, seed restorationBudget-focused homeowners with one clearly defined wet spot
BetterGood, plus downspout tie-ins, a catch basin at the low spot, cleanout access, and sod instead of seedMost homeowners, because it also handles the roof water feeding the problem
BestBetter, plus extended discharge or a dry well, regrading the problem area, and a follow-up inspection after the first heavy rainHomeowners with recurring water near the foundation or who plan to stay long-term

Rules for options:

  • Every option has to fix the problem. A "Good" you expect to fail comes back as a callback or a bad review.
  • Recommend the middle option. Build it as the honest best fit for most yards.
  • Price the steps sensibly. Moving from Good to Better should add clear value for the money.
  • List what each option includes. A short bullet list per option reads better than a paragraph.

Presenting the quote

Emailing a PDF and waiting is the weakest way to sell a drainage job. When you can, present at the end of the site visit or on a short follow-up call.

Presentation order

  1. Restate the problem in their words. "You said the back corner stays soggy for days after rain and you've had water in the basement twice."
  2. Explain the cause. "Your downspouts dump at the foundation and the yard slopes back toward the house here."
  3. Explain the fix. Show how the system collects, carries and discharges water.
  4. Show the three options and what changes between them.
  5. Recommend one. "For your yard I'd go with Better, and here's why."
  6. Show the price. By now they know what they're buying.
  7. Ask for the decision. "Which option makes sense for you?"

When the other quote is cheaper

Don't drop your price on the spot. Ask to compare scopes:

"That's fair, and I want you to get a good deal. Can we look at both quotes side by side so we know it's the same job? Does theirs include the discharge to the curb, restoring the lawn, and fixing any sprinkler lines they cut?"

If the scopes match and theirs is lower, offer a smaller scope (move them to Good) instead of cutting your price on the same work. Cutting price without cutting scope tells the homeowner your first number wasn't real.

Put an expiration on every quote, such as 30 days, and note that material prices can change. It's accurate, and it gives the homeowner a reason to decide.

Quote checklist

  • All five cost buckets filled in, with current material prices
  • Effective per-foot number checked against your normal range
  • Three options, each one a real fix
  • Scope in plain language: footage, depth, discharge point, restoration
  • Exclusions listed (for example, permit fees if required, unknown buried obstructions)
  • Payment terms, deposit and timeline
  • Expiration date
  • Follow-up on your calendar within 48 hours

Holding your price

When you know what a job costs you, you can tell a homeowner who needs a smaller scope from one who is shopping for the lowest number, and you can walk away from the second. Know what each lead and sold job costs you to acquire as well; our post on exclusive vs. shared leads covers that. And before you build a three-option proposal, qualify the lead on the first call.
