Exclusive vs. Shared Leads: The Real Math for Drainage and Irrigation Contractors

The cheapest lead is rarely the cheapest customer. Here's how to compare exclusive and shared leads using the numbers that actually hit your bank account.

Lead StrategyOct 1, 2026·9 min read·By the Launch Lead Gen team

When contractors compare lead sources, they almost always compare the wrong number. They look at cost per lead, see that shared leads are cheaper, and buy those. Then they wonder why their phone is full of homeowners who already booked someone else. The number that matters isn't what you pay for a lead. It's what you pay for a sold job.

Key takeaways

  • Cost per lead is the least useful number. Cost per booked appointment and cost per sale tell the real story.
  • Shared leads are sold to multiple contractors, so you're in a race and often a price war.
  • Exclusive leads cost more up front but can win on cost per sale if your close rate is meaningfully higher.
  • Speed-to-lead matters with both types, and it's make-or-break with shared leads.
  • You can't compare sources without tracking each lead from first contact to paid invoice.

What's the difference?

Shared leads are sold to more than one contractor. A homeowner fills out a form asking about a French drain or a sprinkler repair, and that request goes to several companies at once. Whoever gets there first, or quotes lowest, usually wins.

Exclusive leads go to one contractor only. You're not racing anyone for that specific request. The homeowner may still shop around on their own, but you're not one of four companies calling them in the same ten minutes.

Neither type is automatically good or bad. What matters is how they perform for your business, and you can only know that by doing the math.

The three numbers that matter

  1. Cost per lead (CPL): what you pay for each lead. Easy to see, easy to compare, and misleading on its own.
  2. Cost per booked appointment: total lead spend divided by the number of estimates or site visits you actually booked.
  3. Cost per sale (or cost per acquisition): total lead spend divided by the number of jobs you closed and got paid for.

Cost per sale is the one that shows up on your profit and loss. A lead source with a higher CPL can easily have a lower cost per sale if more of those leads turn into appointments and more of those appointments turn into jobs.

A worked comparison

The numbers below are hypothetical. They're made up to show how the math works. They are not industry averages and not anyone's actual results. Plug in your own numbers to see what's true for your business.

Example scenario: A drainage contractor spends $2,000 a month on leads and wants to compare two options.

Metric (hypothetical)Shared leadsExclusive leads
Cost per lead$40$100
Leads for $2,0005020
Contact rate (reached the homeowner)50% → 25 contacted80% → 16 contacted
Booking rate (of contacted)40% → 10 appointments70% → about 11 appointments
Cost per booked appointment$200about $182
Close rate (of appointments)20% → 2 jobs40% → about 4 jobs
Cost per sale$1,000about $500

In this made-up example, the shared lead is 60% cheaper per lead, but the exclusive lead ends up costing half as much per sold job. Why? Because every step of the funnel is a little better: more homeowners pick up, more agree to a visit, and more say yes when you're not the third quote they've heard that day.

Now flip it. If your close rate on shared leads were just as high as on exclusive leads, shared would win. That's why you have to run the numbers with your own data. The table is a method, not a verdict.

Pro tip: Also compare average job value by source. Price-shopping homeowners on shared leads may push you toward smaller scopes or discounts. If one source closes at a lower average ticket, factor that into your cost per sale versus profit per sale.

Add profit to the picture

Cost per sale tells you what you paid. To know if it was worth it, compare it to gross profit per job.

Example: If your average French drain job brings in $1,800 in gross profit, a $500 cost per sale leaves $1,300. A $1,000 cost per sale leaves $800. Both might be acceptable, but one is clearly better. For irrigation, add expected plan and repair revenue over time to the job value. That changes what you can afford to pay. See our post on building recurring irrigation revenue for how to estimate that, and how to price French drain jobs for the drainage side.

Speed-to-lead: the hidden variable

How fast you respond affects every number in that table. A homeowner who just submitted a request is thinking about their soggy yard or broken sprinklers right now. An hour later, they're at work, picking up kids, or already talking to someone else.

With shared leads, speed is everything. You're competing with other contractors who got the same lead at the same moment. With exclusive leads, you have a bit more breathing room, but slow follow-up still costs you jobs.

A speed-to-lead playbook

  1. Get leads to a phone, not just an inbox. Set up text or push notifications so whoever's on duty sees new leads instantly.
  2. Call first, then text. If they don't pick up, send a short text right away so they know who called.
  3. Use a follow-up sequence. Example: call immediately, text immediately, call again later the same day, call and text the next morning, then a final text on day three.
  4. Assign ownership. One person is responsible for new leads during business hours. Have a plan for evenings and weekends.
  5. Book on the first call. Offer two specific times for the site visit instead of "when works for you?"

First-call script

"Hi, is this [Name]? This is [Your Name] with [Company]. You reached out about [standing water in your backyard / your sprinkler system]. I've got a couple quick questions so I can make sure we send the right person. [Ask 2–3 qualifying questions.] Sounds like something we handle all the time. I can have someone out Tuesday at 10 or Wednesday at 3. Which works better?"

For the qualifying questions, see our guide on qualifying drainage leads.

Text when they don't answer

"Hi [Name], it's [Your Name] with [Company]. Just tried calling about your [drainage / irrigation] request. When's a good time to chat for 2 minutes? Or reply here and I can get you on the schedule."

What to track in your CRM

You can't compare lead sources if you don't know where each job came from. Every lead should be tagged at the moment it comes in and followed all the way to a paid invoice. At minimum, track:

  • Lead source (and whether it was exclusive or shared)
  • Lead cost
  • Date and time received
  • Time of first contact attempt (so you can measure response time)
  • Contacted? yes/no, and how many attempts it took
  • Appointment booked? yes/no, and the date
  • Estimate amount
  • Won or lost, and the reason if lost (price, timing, went with someone else, not a fit, never reached)
  • Invoice amount and gross profit
  • Service type (French drain, grading, sump/discharge, irrigation install, repair, plan)
  • Returned or credited lead, if your lead provider has a return policy, and why

Once a month, pull a simple report by source: total spend, leads, contacted, booked, sold, revenue and profit. Calculate cost per booked appointment and cost per sale. After two or three months, you'll have real answers instead of gut feelings.

Lost-reason codes are gold

If most of your lost shared leads are "went with someone else" or "price," that's a sign you're losing races and price wars. If most of your lost exclusive leads are "never reached," that's a follow-up problem, not a lead problem. The lost reason tells you what to fix.

When shared leads can make sense

To be fair, shared leads aren't useless. They can work if:

  • You have someone dedicated to responding within minutes, all day.
  • You're a low-overhead operation that can compete on price.
  • You're testing a new service area and want volume quickly.
  • Your own tracking shows they produce an acceptable cost per sale.

The point isn't that one type always wins. The point is to judge every source on cost per sale and profit, not on sticker price per lead.

Monthly review checklist

  • Total spend by source
  • Average response time by source
  • Contact rate, booking rate and close rate by source
  • Cost per booked appointment and cost per sale by source
  • Average job value and gross profit by source
  • Top lost reasons by source
  • Seasonal patterns (see the drainage demand calendar)
