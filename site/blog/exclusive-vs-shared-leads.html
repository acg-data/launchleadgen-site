Exclusive vs. Shared Leads for Drainage and Irrigation Contractors

Shared leads cost less per lead, but cost per sale is the number that matters. How to compare lead sources with your own close rates and job profit.

Lead StrategyOct 1, 2026·5 min read·By the Launch Lead Gen team

Most contractors compare lead sources by cost per lead, see that shared leads are cheaper and buy those. Then their phone fills up with homeowners who already booked someone else. Compare sources by what you pay for each sold job.

Key takeaways

  • Cost per booked appointment and cost per sale tell you more than cost per lead.
  • Shared leads go to several contractors, so you're racing them and often competing on price.
  • Exclusive leads cost more up front but can win on cost per sale if your close rate is meaningfully higher.
  • Response speed matters with both types and decides most shared leads.
  • You need to track each lead from first contact to paid invoice to compare sources.

How the two types differ

Shared leads are sold to more than one contractor. A homeowner asks about a French drain or a sprinkler repair, and the request goes to several companies at once. Whoever calls first or quotes lowest usually wins.

Exclusive leads go to one contractor. The homeowner may still shop around, but you're not one of four companies calling in the same ten minutes.

Neither type is automatically better. You find out which works for you by running the numbers.

Three numbers to compare

  1. Cost per lead (CPL): what you pay for each lead. Easy to compare, misleading on its own.
  2. Cost per booked appointment: total lead spend divided by estimates or site visits booked.
  3. Cost per sale: total lead spend divided by jobs closed and paid.

Cost per sale is what shows up on your P&L. A source with a higher CPL can have a lower cost per sale if more of its leads book and more of those appointments close.

Worked comparison

These numbers are hypothetical, made up to show the method. They are not industry averages or anyone's actual results.

Example: A drainage contractor spends $2,000 a month on leads and compares two options.

Metric (hypothetical)Shared leadsExclusive leads
Cost per lead$40$100
Leads for $2,0005020
Contact rate (reached the homeowner)50% → 25 contacted80% → 16 contacted
Booking rate (of contacted)40% → 10 appointments70% → about 11 appointments
Cost per booked appointment$200about $182
Close rate (of appointments)20% → 2 jobs40% → about 4 jobs
Cost per sale$1,000about $500

In this example the shared lead is 60% cheaper per lead, but the exclusive lead costs half as much per sold job, because each step converts a little better: more homeowners pick up, more agree to a visit and more say yes when you aren't the third quote that day.

If your close rate on shared leads matched your exclusive close rate, shared would win. Use the table as a method and plug in your own data.

Compare average job value by source too. Price-shopping homeowners may push you toward smaller scopes or discounts, so a source that closes at a lower ticket can look better on cost per sale than it does on profit.

Adding profit per job

Cost per sale tells you what you paid. Compare it to gross profit per job to see whether it was worth it.

Example: If an average French drain job brings $1,800 in gross profit, a $500 cost per sale leaves $1,300 and a $1,000 cost per sale leaves $800. For irrigation, add expected plan and repair revenue to the job value, which raises what you can afford per sale. See building recurring irrigation revenue for how to estimate that, and how to price French drain jobs for drainage.

Response speed

Response time affects every row in that table. A homeowner who just submitted a request is thinking about their soggy yard or broken sprinklers now. An hour later they're at work, picking up kids or talking to someone else.

With shared leads you're competing with contractors who got the same lead at the same moment, so speed decides most of them. Exclusive leads give you more room, but slow follow-up still loses jobs.

Speed-to-lead steps

  1. Send leads to a phone. Set up text or push alerts so whoever's on duty sees new leads immediately.
  2. Call first, then text. If they don't pick up, text right away so they know who called.
  3. Use a follow-up sequence. For example: call and text immediately, call again later that day, call and text the next morning, then a final text on day three.
  4. Assign ownership. One person owns new leads during business hours, with a plan for evenings and weekends.
  5. Book on the first call. Offer two specific times for the site visit instead of asking what works for them.

First-call script

"Hi, is this [Name]? This is [Your Name] with [Company]. You reached out about [standing water in your backyard / your sprinkler system]. I've got a couple quick questions so I can make sure we send the right person. [Ask 2–3 qualifying questions.] Sounds like something we handle all the time. I can have someone out Tuesday at 10 or Wednesday at 3. Which works better?"

For qualifying questions, see qualifying drainage leads.

Text when they don't answer

"Hi [Name], it's [Your Name] with [Company]. Just tried calling about your [drainage / irrigation] request. When's a good time to chat for 2 minutes? Or reply here and I can get you on the schedule."

What to track in your CRM

Tag every lead with its source when it comes in and follow it to a paid invoice. At minimum, track:

  • Lead source, and whether it was exclusive or shared
  • Lead cost
  • Date and time received
  • Time of first contact attempt, to measure response time
  • Contacted (yes/no) and number of attempts
  • Appointment booked (yes/no) and date
  • Estimate amount
  • Won or lost, with the lost reason (price, timing, went with someone else, not a fit, never reached)
  • Invoice amount and gross profit
  • Service type (French drain, grading, sump/discharge, irrigation install, repair, plan)
  • Returned or credited lead, if your provider has a return policy, and why

Once a month, pull spend, leads, contacted, booked, sold, revenue and profit by source, then calculate cost per booked appointment and cost per sale. After two or three months you'll have real numbers to decide with.

Lost-reason codes

If most lost shared leads are "went with someone else" or "price," you're losing races and price fights. If most lost exclusive leads are "never reached," you have a follow-up problem. The lost reason tells you what to fix.

When shared leads can make sense

Shared leads can work if:

  • You have someone dedicated to responding within minutes all day.
  • You run low overhead and can compete on price.
  • You're testing a new service area and want volume quickly.
  • Your own tracking shows an acceptable cost per sale.

Judge every source on cost per sale and profit, whatever it costs per lead.

Monthly review checklist

  • Total spend by source
  • Average response time by source
  • Contact, booking and close rates by source
  • Cost per booked appointment and cost per sale by source
  • Average job value and gross profit by source
  • Top lost reasons by source
  • Seasonal patterns (see the drainage demand calendar)
