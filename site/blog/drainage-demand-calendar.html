The Drainage Demand Calendar: Plan Marketing, Crews and Leads Around the Seasons

Drainage work doesn't arrive evenly. Use this month-by-month calendar to line up marketing, crews and lead volume before the rush, not after.

DrainageSep 24, 2026·9 min read·By the Launch Lead Gen team

Drainage demand follows the weather. When the snow melts, when storms roll through, and when homeowners start worrying about winter, the phone rings. When it's dry and hot, it gets quiet. The contractors who grow steadily aren't the ones who react hardest when the rain hits. They're the ones who planned their marketing, hiring and lead volume a month or two before it did.

Key takeaways

  • Plan marketing four to eight weeks ahead of each demand spike so you're visible when homeowners start searching.
  • Match what you say to the season: "standing water" and "wet basement" in spring, "storm damage" in summer, "prepare before winter" in fall.
  • Use slow months for training, equipment maintenance, and pre-selling fall and spring work.
  • Ramp lead volume up gradually before peak season and down before slow periods so crews stay steady.

Why a demand calendar matters

Most small drainage companies run on feast or famine. In the busy weeks they can't call leads back fast enough and lose jobs to whoever answers first. In slow weeks crews sit idle while the owner scrambles for work. A simple calendar fixes much of that because it lets you see the next wave coming and prepare for it.

A note before the table: weather and seasons vary a lot by region. A contractor in the upper Midwest deals with deep freeze and a sharp spring thaw. One on the Gulf Coast may see heavy rain much of the year and hurricane season in late summer and fall. One in the Pacific Northwest may have a long, wet fall and winter. The three big waves

Wave 1: Spring thaw (roughly late February to May)

This is usually the biggest wave for contractors in cold climates. Snowmelt plus spring rain saturates the ground, and homeowners walk out to a squishy yard and a damp basement. They're motivated, and many want the job done before they put money into landscaping.

  • Start marketing in February, before the thaw, so you're already showing up when people start searching.
  • Pre-book estimates from your list of past customers and unsold quotes from last fall.
  • Message to use: "Fix the water problem before you invest in your yard this season."

Wave 2: Storm season (roughly June to September)

Summer demand tends to come in bursts tied to heavy storms. A dry stretch can be quiet, and then one big storm floods dozens of yards in your area overnight.

  • Keep a lower base marketing level and a plan to increase it quickly after a significant storm.
  • Have a storm-response text ready for past customers and open estimates: "Big rain last night. If you saw water pooling, now's a great time to get it looked at while you can see exactly where it goes."
  • Leave flex time in the schedule so you can see storm callers within a few days.

Wave 3: Fall prep (roughly September to November)

Homeowners who suffered through a wet spring or a stormy summer often decide to fix it before winter. There's built-in urgency: once the ground freezes, the job waits until spring.

  • Start fall messaging in August.
  • Use the deadline honestly: "We're booking our last installs before the ground freezes."
  • Collect spring pre-bookings from anyone you can't fit in before winter.

Matching lead volume to crew capacity

Buying or generating more leads than you can handle wastes money and hurts your reputation because callbacks get slow. Buying too few in peak season leaves money on the table. A simple way to plan:

  1. Figure out your weekly install capacity. Example: one crew can complete about 3 average drainage jobs per week.
  2. Work backward with your own close rate. Example: if you close 1 in 4 qualified estimates, you need about 12 estimates per week to fill 3 jobs.
  3. Account for leads that don't become estimates. Example: if 3 out of 4 leads turn into a site visit after your first call, you need about 16 leads per week.
  4. Adjust by season. Ramp up toward that number a few weeks before each wave, then taper as your backlog fills.

These numbers are illustrations. Plug in your own close rate, visit rate and crew capacity. If you don't track them yet, start now. And make sure your first call is doing its job; our first-call playbook covers exactly that.

Worked example: cost per job by season

Example: if a lead costs $85, 3 out of 4 leads book a visit, and you close 1 in 4 visits, you need about 5.3 leads per sold job, or roughly $453 in lead cost per job. If your average drainage job is several thousand dollars, that can work well. If your close rate drops in a slow month because homeowners are less urgent, your cost per job rises. That's why many contractors pull back lead spend in the slowest months and push hardest when urgency is high.

Staffing tips for a seasonal business

  • Keep a core crew year-round and add seasonal help for peak waves. Your core people train the new ones.
  • Recruit before you need people. Start in January or February for spring and in July for fall.
  • Cross-train on irrigation work. Drainage and irrigation complement each other. Sprinkler startups in spring and winterizations in fall can keep crews busy when drainage slows. See our post on building recurring irrigation revenue.
  • Protect the office side. In peak season, the bottleneck is often answering phones and scheduling, not digging. Have someone dedicated to calling leads back quickly.
  • Use slow weeks deliberately: equipment repairs, safety training, updating your price sheets, and following up on old estimates.
Pro tip: Before each wave, review your pricing. Material costs move, and peak season is when you have the most leverage to hold your price. Our guide on pricing French drain jobs can help.

Your quarterly planning checklist

  • Shift the calendar months to match your local weather
  • Set target weekly lead volume for each month based on crew capacity
  • Schedule marketing start dates four to eight weeks before each wave
  • Write seasonal messages (thaw, storm, fall prep) and storm-response texts
  • Set recruiting dates for seasonal help
  • Plan equipment maintenance for slow months
  • Build a spring pre-booking list from fall estimates you couldn't schedule
