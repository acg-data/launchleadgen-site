Eight Questions to Qualify a Drainage Lead on the First Call

The first phone call decides whether a drainage lead becomes a site visit or a wasted afternoon. Use these eight questions, the script and the red flags.

DrainageSep 3, 2026·5 min read·By the Launch Lead Gen team

Every drainage lead costs you ad spend, a lead fee or a wasted drive to a yard that was never going to be a job. A five-minute first call tells you whether the homeowner has a real problem and can sign, and it should end with a site visit on the calendar.

Key takeaways

  • Call new leads within minutes, before they book someone else.
  • Ask the same eight questions every time so you can compare leads and train others to take calls.
  • Watch for red flags: no decision-maker, renters, no actual drainage problem, or "just give me a ballpark."
  • Don't end a good call without a specific date and time for the site visit.

Before you dial

Have your calendar open, a notes template ready and the lead details in front of you. Read the form first so the homeowner doesn't have to repeat it, and open with what they wrote: "I see you mentioned water pooling near the back patio. Tell me more about that."

If you're on a job site, have the office or an answering service make the first call with this script.

The eight questions

1. The water problem

Ask: "What's going on with the water? Walk me through it." Let them talk, and listen for where the water collects, how long it sits and what it damages. A wet basement is more urgent than a soggy lawn.

2. History

Ask: "How long has this been happening, and is it getting worse?" A new problem after a storm or a neighbor's construction is different from a years-long one. "Getting worse" signals urgency and often a bigger scope.

3. Previous fixes

Ask: "Has anyone looked at it or tried to fix it?" A failed DIY drain or another contractor's clogged system means careful diagnosis. It also tells you whether they're collecting bids now.

4. Ownership

Ask: "Do you own the home?" Renters usually can't authorize drainage work, so get the owner's or property manager's contact details before booking.

5. Decision-makers

Ask: "Who else would be involved in deciding on the work?" If there's a spouse or co-owner, get them to the visit. Jobs stall when the person who saw your presentation has to talk it over with someone who didn't.

6. Timing

Ask: "When are you hoping to have this taken care of?" "Before the next rain" and "maybe next year" can both be worth a visit, but they get different slots on your calendar.

7. Budget

Ask: "Have you thought about a budget range?" Many won't have a number. The point is to set expectations. If they say "a few hundred bucks" and the job is clearly a French drain, tell them now: "Drainage projects that fix this kind of problem usually run into the thousands, depending on the yard and the area. I'll give you options when I see it. Still want me to take a look?"

8. Site details

Ask: "Is there anything about the property I should know before I come out?" Gates, dogs, tight side-yard access, sprinklers, septic, new landscaping, HOA rules. These affect equipment access and the quote.

Phone script

Change the wording so it sounds like you, but keep the order: rapport, the problem, qualifying, then booking.

Opening: "Hi, is this [Name]? This is [Your Name] with [Company]. You reached out about a drainage problem in your yard. Is now a good time for a few quick questions?"
Problem: "I see you mentioned [detail from form]. Walk me through what's going on with the water."

Let them talk, then work through questions 2 to 8 in conversation instead of reading them like a survey.

Booking: "That helps. It sounds like [short summary, e.g. 'water from the downspouts and the slope is collecting by the patio']. Next step is for me to come out, look at the grade and where the water goes, and give you a few priced options. It takes 30 to 45 minutes. I have [Day] at [Time] or [Day] at [Time]. Which works better? And can [spouse/co-owner] be there too?"
Confirmation: "You're booked for [Day, Time]. I'll text a confirmation to this number. If it rains before then, take a couple of photos or a short video of where the water goes."

The photo request gets the homeowner involved and gives you better diagnostic information.

A red flag tells you what you're walking into so you can decide whether it's worth the drive.

Scoring the lead

Score each lead after you hang up:

  • A lead: Owner, water problem causing damage, wants it fixed within weeks, decision-makers will be at the visit.
  • B lead: Real problem, but timing is loose or budget expectations need work.
  • C lead: Unclear problem, no decision-maker, or "just curious."

A leads get your earliest visit slots. C leads get a follow-up text and a visit only if your calendar has room.

Booking the site visit

  1. Offer two specific times. "Tuesday at 4 or Thursday at 10?" works better than "When works for you?"
  2. Confirm the address and any gate or access details.
  3. Text a confirmation right away with your name, date and time.
  4. Send a reminder the day before, with an easy way to reschedule.
  5. Ask for photos or video of the problem area, especially after rain.
  6. Show up on time, or call before you're late.
Bring your pricing worksheet to the visit. Presenting options on site skips the "I'll email you a quote" step where a lot of jobs go cold. Our guide on pricing French drain jobs shows how to build them.

Worked example of time saved

This is an illustration with made-up numbers. Say you get 20 drainage leads in a month and visit all of them unqualified. At about 1.5 hours per visit with the drive, that's 30 hours. If five-minute calls screen out 5 of them, you spend under two hours on the phone and save about 7.5 hours.
