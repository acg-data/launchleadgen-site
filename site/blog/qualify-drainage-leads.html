The First-Call Playbook: 8 Questions to Qualify a Drainage Lead in Under 5 Minutes

The first phone call decides whether a drainage lead turns into a booked site visit or a wasted afternoon. Here are the 8 questions, the script, and the red flags.

DrainageSep 3, 2026·8 min read·By the Launch Lead Gen team

Every drainage lead costs you something, whether it's ad spend, a lead fee, or just the hour you lose driving to a yard that was never going to become a job. The first phone call is where you protect that time. A tight five-minute call tells you whether the homeowner has a real problem, can make a decision, and is ready to move, and it ends with a site visit on the calendar instead of "I'll call you back."

Key takeaways

  • Call fast. The longer a lead waits, the more likely they have already booked someone else.
  • Use the same 8 questions every time so you can compare leads and train others to take calls.
  • Listen for red flags: no decision-maker, no real problem, renters, or "just want a ballpark."
  • Never end a good call without a specific site visit date and time.

Before you dial

Have three things ready: your calendar open, a notes template (paper or your CRM), and the lead details in front of you. Read what the homeowner already told you on the form so you don't make them repeat themselves. Nothing makes you sound more professional than "I see you mentioned water pooling near the back patio. Tell me more about that."

Pro tip: Call new leads as soon as you can, ideally within minutes. If you're on a job site, have someone in the office or a call service do the first call using this exact script.

The 8 questions

1. "What's going on with the water? Walk me through it."

Open-ended on purpose. Let them talk. You're listening for where the water collects, how long it sits, and what it's damaging: lawn, patio, basement, foundation, garden beds. Someone with water in the basement has more urgency than someone with a soggy corner of lawn.

2. "How long has this been happening, and is it getting worse?"

New problems after a big storm or a neighbor's construction are different from a slow, years-long issue. "Getting worse" signals urgency and often a bigger scope.

3. "Has anyone looked at it or tried to fix it before?"

This tells you a lot. A failed DIY attempt or a previous contractor's drain that clogged means you'll need to diagnose carefully. It also tells you whether they're actively collecting bids right now.

4. "Do you own the home?"

Ask it simply and politely. Renters usually can't authorize drainage work. If they're renters, ask for the owner or property manager's contact info rather than booking a visit with someone who can't sign.

5. "Who else would be involved in deciding on the work?"

If there's a spouse or co-owner, you want them at the site visit. Presenting to one person who then has to "talk it over" with someone who never saw the yard is how good jobs stall.

6. "When are you hoping to have this taken care of?"

Separates "before the next rain" from "maybe next year." Both can be worth a visit, but they get different priority on your calendar.

7. "Have you thought about a budget range for this?"

Many homeowners won't have a number, and that's fine. The goal is to set expectations. If they say "a few hundred bucks" and the job is clearly a multi-thousand-dollar French drain, gently tell them now. Something like: "Most of the drainage projects we do that solve this kind of problem land in the thousands, not hundreds, depending on the yard. Prices vary by job, and I'll give you options when I see it. Does that still make sense to look at?"

8. "Is there anything about the property I should know before I come out?"

Gates, dogs, tight side-yard access, sprinkler system, septic, recent landscaping, HOA rules. This saves surprises on the visit and helps you plan equipment access in your quote.

The phone script

Adjust the wording to sound like you, but keep the order. It moves from rapport to problem to qualification to booking.

"Hi, is this [Name]? This is [Your Name] with [Company]. You reached out about a drainage problem in your yard. Is now an okay time for a few quick questions so I can figure out the best way to help?"
"Great. I see you mentioned [detail from form]. Walk me through what's going on with the water."

Let them talk. Then work through questions 2 to 8 naturally, not like a survey.

"Okay, that's helpful. Based on what you're describing, it sounds like [short summary, e.g. 'water from the downspouts and the slope is collecting by the patio']. The best next step is for me to come out, look at the grade and where the water's going, and give you a few options with clear pricing. It takes about 30 to 45 minutes. I have [Day] at [Time] or [Day] at [Time]. Which works better? And can [spouse/co-owner] be there too?"
"Perfect, you're booked for [Day, Time]. I'll send a text confirmation to this number. If it rains before then, snap a couple of photos or a short video of where the water goes. That really helps."

That last line is gold. It gets the homeowner invested and gives you better diagnostic information.

A red flag doesn't automatically mean "don't go." It means know what you're walking into and decide whether it's worth the drive.

Score the lead in 10 seconds

After you hang up, give the lead a quick score so you can prioritize your week. A simple version:

  • A lead: Owner, real water problem causing damage, wants it fixed within weeks, decision-makers will be at the visit.
  • B lead: Real problem, but timing is loose or budget expectations need work.
  • C lead: Unclear problem, no decision-maker, or "just curious."

A leads get your soonest visit slots. C leads get a polite follow-up text and only a visit if your calendar is open.

Booking the site visit: the details that prevent no-shows

  1. Offer two specific times. "Tuesday at 4 or Thursday at 10?" beats "When works for you?"
  2. Confirm the address and gate or access details.
  3. Send a text confirmation right away with your name, company, date and time.
  4. Send a reminder the day before, with an easy way to reschedule.
  5. Ask for photos or video of the problem area, especially after rain.
  6. Show up on time, or call before you're late. It's your first proof that you're reliable.
Pro tip: Bring your pricing worksheet to the site visit. If you can present good/better/best options on the spot, you skip the "I'll email you a quote" step where many jobs go cold. Our guide on pricing French drain jobs walks through building those options.

Worked example: why five minutes matters

Here's an illustration, not a benchmark. Say you get 20 drainage leads in a month and you drive out to all of them without qualifying. If each visit costs you about 1.5 hours including drive time, that's 30 hours. Now say a five-minute call screens out 5 of those leads as renters, wrong trade, or not serious. You've spent under two hours on calls and saved roughly 7.5 hours of driving and walking yards, time you can spend on the jobs that are actually going to close.

Make the playbook a habit

Print the 8 questions and keep them by the phone or in your truck. Use them on every call for a month and you'll notice your site visits get better, your quotes close more often, and you stop burning afternoons. If you're planning for the busy season, pair this with our drainage demand calendar so you know when call volume is about to spike.
