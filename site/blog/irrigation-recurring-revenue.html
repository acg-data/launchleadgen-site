Turn One Sprinkler Install Into a 10-Year Customer: Building Recurring Irrigation Revenue

The install is the start of the relationship, not the end. Here's how to build maintenance plans, scheduling routes and renewal scripts that keep a customer on your books for a decade.

IrrigationSep 3, 2026·8 min read·By the Launch Lead Gen team

Most irrigation contractors treat the install as the finish line. The trench is backfilled, the controller is programmed, the invoice is paid, and the customer disappears until something breaks. Then they call whoever shows up first on a search. That's a lot of money left on the lawn. Every system you put in the ground needs a spring start-up, needs adjusting mid-season, and in freeze-prone areas needs to be blown out every fall. If you don't own that work, somebody else will.

Key takeaways

  • Every install creates at least two or three predictable service visits per year. Sell them before you leave the job.
  • Three plan tiers (basic, standard, premium) make it easy for homeowners to say yes to something.
  • Route your plan customers by zip code so service visits are fast and profitable.
  • Auto-renew with a heads-up notice beats chasing renewals one by one.
  • Track plan count and renewal rate as closely as you track new installs.

Why recurring revenue changes your business

New installs are lumpy. You get a rush in spring, a slowdown in the heat, and a scramble for work in fall. Maintenance plans smooth that out. They give you a base of booked work before the season starts, and they keep your techs busy during the weeks when new sales are slow.

They also make every lead you buy worth more. If an install lead turns into one job, you earn the install margin once. If it turns into an install plus several years of service visits, repairs and upgrades, that same lead pays you back again and again.

The visits every system needs

  • Spring start-up: Charge the system slowly, check for freeze damage, inspect heads and valves, set the controller for the season.
  • Mid-season audit: Walk each zone, fix misaligned or clogged heads, check coverage, adjust run times for summer heat and any local watering rules.
  • Fall winterization: In freeze-prone regions, blow out lines with compressed air, shut off and protect the backflow, put the controller in off or rain mode.
  • Repairs as needed: Broken heads from mowers, leaking valves, wire problems, controller failures.

If you're in a warm climate where winterization isn't a thing, swap the fall visit for a second audit or a pre-dry-season check. The structure still works.

Building your plan tiers

Don't offer one plan. Offer three. When homeowners see a single price, they decide yes or no. When they see three options, they usually decide which one. Keep the tiers simple and easy to explain in under a minute.

TierWhat's includedWho it's for
BasicSpring start-up and fall winterizationHomeowners who just want the system turned on and protected
StandardBasic plus one mid-season audit, priority scheduling, small discount on repair partsMost customers. This should be your default recommendation.
PremiumStandard plus a second audit, a set amount of minor repair labor included, controller seasonal adjustments, first-in-line service callsLarger properties, landscape-proud owners, people who never want to think about it

A pricing structure example

Prices vary a lot by region, system size and your labor cost, so treat these as a worked example of how to structure pricing, not as numbers to copy.

Example: Say your standalone prices are $95 for a spring start-up, $90 for a winterization and $110 for an audit, for a typical residential system up to a set number of zones. Bought one at a time, the standard tier's three visits would cost the homeowner $295. You could price the plan at around $250 to $265. The homeowner saves real money, and you get the work locked in, scheduled on your terms, and paid in advance or monthly.

For premium, add the second audit ($110 standalone) and an allowance of minor repair labor. If you price premium in the $375 to $425 range in this example, you're still ahead because plan visits are routed tightly and most of the "included repair labor" is quick head swaps you'd be doing during the visit anyway.

Two rules that keep plans profitable:

  • Set a zone limit. Base pricing covers up to a certain number of zones. Add a per-zone charge beyond that. A 14-zone estate is not the same job as a 5-zone tract home.
  • Parts are extra. Include labor for minor repairs on premium if you want, but bill parts separately. Otherwise one bad valve can wipe out the plan margin.
Pro tip: Offer monthly billing as well as annual. Many homeowners will say yes to a small monthly charge faster than one larger payment, and it keeps your cash flow steadier through the off-season.

When and how to sell the plan

The best time to sell a maintenance plan is at the end of the install, when the customer is standing in their yard watching the system run for the first time. They've just spent real money. They want it protected. You're the person who built it.

Install walkthrough script

"Before I head out, let me show you a couple things. This is your backflow, this is your controller, and here's how each zone is set. Now, a system like this needs a start-up every spring and needs to be blown out before the first freeze, or you can crack pipes and fittings. Most of our customers put that on a plan so they never have to remember. The standard plan covers spring, fall and a mid-summer check where we adjust heads and run times. It's [price] a year, or [monthly price] a month. Want me to put you on the schedule now so you're covered this fall?"

Note what this does. It explains the risk in plain language, frames the plan as what most customers do, and asks for a decision right now. Don't email a brochure and hope.

Selling to customers who didn't buy an install from you

Plenty of homeowners have systems somebody else put in. Repair calls, start-up requests and audit leads are all chances to convert them. After any service call, use a version of this:

"Everything's running now. I found [two broken heads / a leaking valve], which is pretty common when a system hasn't been checked in a while. If you'd like, I can put you on our plan so we catch this stuff every spring before it costs you water or dead grass. Today's visit would count toward it."

Crediting part of today's visit toward the plan is a strong closer. It turns a one-time ticket into a recurring customer with almost no extra effort.

Scheduling: where plan profit is made or lost

A maintenance plan only makes money if your techs aren't burning hours driving across town for a 40-minute visit. Plan customers give you something new installs never do: control over when the work happens.

  1. Group by zip code or neighborhood. Build routes so a tech can knock out several plan visits in one area in a day.
  2. Open windows, not exact days. Tell plan customers "your start-up will happen the week of April 14" and confirm the day a few days ahead. That gives you room to route.
  3. Book next visit at the current visit. When a tech finishes a spring start-up, the mid-season audit window should already be in the system.
  4. Protect fall capacity. Winterization season is short and weather-driven. Plan customers get booked first, then open slots go to one-off customers at standalone rates.
  5. Use the visit to spot upgrades. Audits are the natural time to recommend a smart controller, drip conversion, or zone additions. See our guide on selling water-saving upgrades.

Renewals: make them automatic

The biggest leak in most maintenance programs is renewal. A plan ends, nobody calls, and the customer drifts. The fix is simple: make plans auto-renew by default, with clear notice and an easy way to cancel. Spell out the auto-renew terms when they sign up so there are no surprises, and follow any local consumer rules on recurring billing.

Renewal notice (email or text)

"Hi [Name], it's [Your Company]. Your irrigation plan renews on [date] at [price]. Nothing for you to do. We'll reach out the week before your spring start-up to confirm a day. Last year we [replaced 3 heads / adjusted run times in July / winterized on Oct 28]. Want to make any changes to your plan? Just reply here."

Mentioning what you actually did last year reminds them the plan is working, which is the main reason people cancel: they forget what they're paying for.

Win-back script for cancelled customers

"Hi [Name], we haven't had you on the schedule this year. Just a reminder that the first freeze usually comes around [typical local timeframe]. If you want us to blow out your lines, I've got openings in your neighborhood the week of [date]."

A worked example: what one customer is worth

This is a hypothetical example to show the math, not a promise of results.

Example: You install a system with a $1,500 gross profit. The customer signs up for a standard plan at $260 a year, and you net about $120 of that after labor and drive time. They also average one paid repair a year with about $100 of profit. Over ten years, that's the $1,500 install plus roughly $2,200 in plan and repair profit, before counting any upgrades like a smart controller or a drip conversion. The install was less than half the value of that customer.

Now run your own numbers. If you know what a customer is really worth over time, you can make better decisions about how much to spend to get one.

What to track

  • Plan attach rate: what share of installs and service calls convert to a plan.
  • Active plan count by tier, every month.
  • Renewal rate: how many plans renew each year.
  • Revenue per plan customer including repairs and upgrades.
  • Average drive time per plan visit. If it creeps up, your routes need work.

Recurring revenue starts with the first install, and the first install starts with a homeowner who actually wants a system.
