Building Recurring Irrigation Revenue From Every Install

Every system you install needs a start-up, an audit and usually a blowout every year. How to set up plan tiers, routes and renewal scripts so that work stays with you.

IrrigationSep 3, 2026·6 min read·By the Launch Lead Gen team

Most irrigation contractors finish the install, collect the check and don't hear from the customer again until something breaks. By then the homeowner calls whoever comes up first in a search. Every system you put in needs a spring start-up, a mid-season adjustment and, in freeze-prone areas, a fall blowout. If you don't book that work, someone else will.

Key takeaways

  • Every install creates two or three predictable service visits a year. Sell them before you leave the job.
  • Offer three plan tiers so the homeowner picks one instead of deciding yes or no.
  • Route plan customers by ZIP code so visits stay short and profitable.
  • Auto-renew with advance notice instead of chasing renewals one at a time.
  • Track plan count and renewal rate as closely as new installs.

Why recurring revenue matters

Install work is lumpy: a spring rush, a slow stretch in the heat, then a scramble in fall. Maintenance plans give you booked work before the season starts and keep techs busy when new sales slow down.

They also raise the value of every lead you buy. An install lead that turns into one job pays you once. One that turns into an install plus years of service visits, repairs and upgrades keeps paying.

Visits every system needs

  • Spring start-up: Charge the system slowly, check for freeze damage, inspect heads and valves, set the controller for the season.
  • Mid-season audit: Walk each zone, fix misaligned or clogged heads, check coverage, adjust run times for heat and local watering rules.
  • Fall winterization: In freeze-prone regions, blow out lines with compressed air, shut off and protect the backflow, put the controller in off or rain mode.
  • Repairs as needed: Mower-damaged heads, leaking valves, wire problems, failed controllers.

In warm climates without winterization, replace the fall visit with a second audit or a pre-dry-season check.

Plan tiers

Offer three plans. With one price, homeowners decide yes or no. With three, they usually pick one. You should be able to explain all three in under a minute.

TierWhat's includedWho it's for
BasicSpring start-up and fall winterizationHomeowners who just want the system turned on and protected
StandardBasic plus one mid-season audit, priority scheduling, small discount on repair partsMost customers. Make this your default recommendation.
PremiumStandard plus a second audit, a set amount of minor repair labor, seasonal controller adjustments, first-in-line service callsLarger properties and owners who never want to think about it

Pricing example

Prices vary by region, system size and labor cost. The numbers below show how to structure pricing, not what to charge.

Example: Say your standalone prices are $95 for a start-up, $90 for a winterization and $110 for an audit, for a residential system up to a set number of zones. Bought separately, the standard tier's three visits cost $295. You might price the plan at $250 to $265. The homeowner saves money, and you get the work booked in advance, scheduled on your terms and paid up front or monthly.

For premium, add the second audit ($110 standalone) and a minor repair labor allowance. In this example, $375 to $425 still leaves you ahead, because plan visits are tightly routed and most included repair labor is quick head swaps you'd do during the visit anyway.

Two rules keep plans profitable:

  • Set a zone limit. Base pricing covers up to a certain number of zones, with a per-zone charge beyond that. A 14-zone estate is a different job from a 5-zone tract home.
  • Bill parts separately. Include minor repair labor on premium if you like, but charge for parts, or one bad valve wipes out the plan margin.
Offer monthly billing as well as annual. Many homeowners agree to a small monthly charge faster than one larger payment, and it steadies your cash flow through the off-season.

Selling the plan at the install

The best time to sell a plan is at the end of the install, while the customer watches the system run for the first time. They just spent real money, they want it protected, and you built it.

Install walkthrough script

"Before I head out, let me show you a couple things. This is your backflow, this is your controller, and here's how each zone is set. A system like this needs a start-up every spring and needs to be blown out before the first freeze, or you can crack pipes and fittings. Most of our customers put that on a plan so they never have to remember. The standard plan covers spring, fall and a mid-summer check where we adjust heads and run times. It's [price] a year, or [monthly price] a month. Want me to put you on the schedule now so you're covered this fall?"

The script explains the risk plainly, presents the plan as what most customers do and asks for a decision on the spot. Don't email a brochure and hope.

Selling to customers with someone else's system

Repair calls, start-up requests and audit leads from homeowners with existing systems are all chances to sign plans. After any service call:

"Everything's running now. I found [two broken heads / a leaking valve], which is pretty common when a system hasn't been checked in a while. If you'd like, I can put you on our plan so we catch this every spring before it costs you water or dead grass. Today's visit would count toward it."

Crediting part of today's visit toward the plan closes a lot of these.

Scheduling plan visits

A plan only makes money if techs aren't driving across town for a 40-minute visit. Plan customers give you something installs don't: control over when the work happens.

  1. Group by ZIP code or neighborhood. Build routes so a tech can finish several plan visits in one area in a day.
  2. Give windows, not exact days. Tell customers "your start-up will happen the week of April 14" and confirm the day a few days ahead.
  3. Book the next visit at the current one. When a tech finishes a start-up, the audit window should already be scheduled.
  4. Protect fall capacity. Winterization season is short and weather-driven. Book plan customers first, then sell open slots to one-off customers at standalone rates.
  5. Spot upgrades during audits. Audits are the natural time to recommend a smart controller, drip conversion or added zones. See selling water-saving upgrades.

Renewals

Most maintenance programs lose customers at renewal. The plan ends, nobody calls, and the customer drifts. Make plans auto-renew by default, with clear notice and an easy way to cancel. Spell out the terms at sign-up and follow any local rules on recurring billing.

Renewal notice (email or text)

"Hi [Name], it's [Your Company]. Your irrigation plan renews on [date] at [price]. Nothing for you to do. We'll reach out the week before your spring start-up to confirm a day. Last year we [replaced 3 heads / adjusted run times in July / winterized on Oct 28]. Want to make any changes to your plan? Just reply here."

Listing last year's work matters because people usually cancel when they forget what they're paying for.

Win-back text for cancelled customers

"Hi [Name], we haven't had you on the schedule this year. Just a reminder that the first freeze usually comes around [typical local timeframe]. If you want us to blow out your lines, I've got openings in your neighborhood the week of [date]."

Worked example: lifetime value of one customer

This is a hypothetical example to show the math, not a promise of results.

Example: You install a system with $1,500 gross profit. The customer signs up for a standard plan at $260 a year, and you net about $120 of that after labor and drive time. They average one paid repair a year at about $100 profit. Over ten years that's the $1,500 install plus roughly $2,200 in plan and repair profit, before any upgrades. The install was less than half of what that customer was worth.

Run your own numbers. Once you know what a customer is worth over time, you know how much you can spend to get one.

What to track

  • Plan attach rate: share of installs and service calls that convert to a plan.
  • Active plans: count by tier, every month.
  • Renewal rate: share of plans that renew each year.
  • Revenue per plan customer: including repairs and upgrades.
  • Drive time per plan visit: if it creeps up, rework your routes.

If you want more irrigation calls in your area, check whether your ZIP codes are open.
