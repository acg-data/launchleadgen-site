How to Price French Drain Jobs So You Win Bids Without Racing to the Bottom

Stop guessing at a per-foot number and hoping it sticks. Build your drainage price from real costs, offer three options, and present it so the homeowner compares value, not just the bottom line.

DrainageAug 14, 2026·9 min read·By the Launch Lead Gen team

Most drainage contractors lose bids for one of two reasons: they priced too high without explaining why, or they priced too low and won a job that ate their margin. Racing the cheapest guy in town is a losing game. The way out is to build your number from real costs, package it so the homeowner has choices, and present it in a way that makes your quote the easy yes, even when it isn't the lowest.

Key takeaways

  • Build every price from five cost buckets: labor, materials, equipment, disposal/restoration, and overhead plus profit.
  • Use per-linear-foot pricing as an internal check, not as the number you lead with.
  • Offer good/better/best options so the homeowner chooses between your packages instead of between you and a competitor.
  • Present the quote in person or on a call, walk through the problem first, and put the price last.

Start with the five cost buckets

If you only remember a per-foot number from a past job, you are pricing from memory, not from math. Every French drain or yard drainage job breaks down into the same five buckets. Write them down for every bid, even the small ones, until it becomes automatic.

1. Labor

Estimate crew hours honestly: layout and locates, digging or trenching, laying fabric and pipe, gravel, backfill, cleanup. Multiply hours by your loaded labor rate, meaning wages plus payroll taxes, workers' comp and any benefits. A crew member you pay $22 an hour can easily cost you noticeably more once the extras are added. If you don't know your loaded rate, figure it out before your next bid.

2. Materials

Perforated pipe, solid pipe for discharge runs, filter fabric, washed stone, fittings, catch basins, pop-up emitters, dry well components. Price from current supplier invoices, not last year's. Add a waste factor for stone and fabric because you will almost always use a bit more than the plan says.

3. Equipment

Mini excavator, trencher, compact track loader, dump trailer. Even if you own the equipment, charge for it. Owned machines have payments, maintenance, fuel and wear. Set an hourly or daily internal rate per machine and include it on every job that uses it.

4. Disposal and restoration

This bucket gets forgotten most often, and it is where profit quietly disappears. Spoil removal and dump fees, sod or seed replacement, re-laying pavers you had to pull, repairing irrigation lines you cut through. If the yard has a sprinkler system, assume you will hit at least one line and budget for the repair.

5. Overhead and profit

Overhead is everything that keeps the business running when no one is digging: truck payments, insurance, phone, software, office help, marketing and lead costs. Profit is separate. It is the reward for the risk you take and the money that lets you buy the next machine. Don't let a homeowner's pushback convince you profit is optional.

Pro tip: Include your marketing cost per sold job in overhead. If you spend money to get leads, that cost is real and belongs in your price, just like stone and pipe.

Per-linear-foot vs. job-based pricing

Plenty of homeowners will call and ask, "What do you charge per foot?" It is a fair question, and it is a trap if you answer it with a single number.

ApproachWhere it helpsWhere it hurts
Per linear footQuick internal sanity check; easy for simple, open-yard runsIgnores access, depth, soil, discharge, restoration; invites apples-to-oranges comparisons
Job-based (scope price)Captures the real cost of the specific site; easier to defendTakes more time to build; needs a clear scope so the homeowner understands it

The best approach for most contractors is to price the job from the five buckets, then divide by the footage to see your effective per-foot number. If it lands far outside what you normally see in your market, look again. Either the site really is unusual (tight access, rocky soil, deep pipe, long discharge run) or you missed something.

Worked example

This is an illustration only. Your costs and regional pricing will be different.

  • Run: 90 feet of French drain along a side yard, discharging to the curb
  • Labor: 3-person crew, 1.5 days, 36 total hours at a $40 loaded rate = $1,440
  • Materials: pipe, fabric, stone, fittings, curb outlet = $1,050
  • Equipment: mini excavator, 1.5 days at an internal rate of $300/day = $450
  • Disposal and restoration: spoil haul, seed and straw, one irrigation repair = $400
  • Direct cost subtotal: $3,340
  • Overhead at 20% of direct cost: $668
  • Profit at 15% of cost plus overhead: about $601
  • Price: about $4,610, or roughly $51 per foot effective

Now imagine a competitor quotes "$35 a foot." On paper that's $3,150. If you only ever talked per foot, you'd look $1,460 more expensive. If you talked scope, you might find the competitor isn't including the curb outlet, restoration, or the irrigation repair, and the homeowner will discover those later.

Build good, better, best options

When you give one price, the homeowner's only decision is "this contractor or another one." When you give three, the decision becomes "which of these options do I want?" That is a much better conversation to be in.

OptionWhat it includesWho it's for
GoodFrench drain along the problem area, standard pipe and stone, discharge to the nearest acceptable point, basic seed restorationBudget-focused homeowners with a clearly defined wet spot
BetterEverything in Good, plus downspout tie-ins, a catch basin at the low spot, cleanout access, and sod instead of seedMost homeowners; solves the source, not just the symptom
BestEverything in Better, plus extended discharge or dry well, regrading of the problem area, and a written follow-up inspection after the first heavy rainHomeowners with recurring water near the foundation or who plan to stay long-term

A few rules for making options work:

  • Every option must actually solve the problem. Never offer a "Good" that you know will fail. It will come back to you as a callback or a bad review.
  • Make the middle option the one you recommend. Build it as the honest best fit for most yards.
  • Price the gaps sensibly. The jump from Good to Better should feel like a lot of added value for the money.
  • Name what is included, not just the price. A bullet list per option is clearer than a paragraph.

Present the quote so price comes last

Emailing a PDF with a number at the bottom and waiting is the weakest way to sell a drainage job. Whenever you can, present the quote in person at the end of the site visit or on a short follow-up call.

A simple presentation order

  1. Restate the problem in their words. "You told me the back corner stays soggy for days after rain and you've had water in the basement twice."
  2. Explain the cause. "Your downspouts dump right at the foundation and the yard slopes back toward the house here."
  3. Explain the fix. Walk through how the system collects, carries and discharges water.
  4. Show the three options. Point out what changes between each.
  5. Recommend one. "For your yard I'd go with Better. Here's why."
  6. Then show the price. By now they understand what they're buying.
  7. Ask for the decision. "Which option makes sense for you?"

Handling "the other guy was cheaper"

Don't drop your price on the spot. Ask to compare scopes instead:

"That's fair, and I want you to get a good deal. Can we look at what's included side by side? I want to make sure you're comparing the same job. Does their quote include the discharge to the curb, restoring the lawn, and fixing any sprinkler lines they cut?"

If the scopes really match and theirs is lower, you can decide whether to adjust the scope (move them to Good) rather than cutting your price on the same work. Cutting price without cutting scope teaches homeowners that your first number wasn't real.

Pro tip: Put a clear expiration on every quote, such as 30 days, and note that material prices can change. It's honest, and it gives a gentle reason to decide.

Quote checklist before you send it

  • All five cost buckets filled in, with current material prices
  • Effective per-foot number checked against your normal range
  • Three options, each one a real fix
  • Scope written in plain language: footage, depth, discharge point, restoration
  • Exclusions listed (for example, permit fees if required, unknown buried obstructions)
  • Payment terms, deposit and timeline
  • Expiration date
  • Follow-up scheduled on your calendar within 48 hours

Know your numbers so you can hold your price

Confidence comes from knowing your costs. When you know exactly what a job costs you, you can tell the difference between a homeowner who needs a smaller scope and one who is just shopping for the lowest number. You can walk away from the second kind without worrying. It also helps to know what each lead and sold job really costs you to acquire.
